# The Better You Get at Dating Apps, the Worse the Date Can Get
> **Example report.** Included to demonstrate a downstream use of the research harness. Sources are listed for inspection; this file is not itself a certification of every claim.

*AI can write the profile, choose the match and improve the message. Unfortunately, somebody still has to show up.*

Online dating has acquired a peculiar new problem.

The machines are getting better at it.

In one experiment involving 168 adults, researchers asked people either to write their own message requesting a date, use AI to write it, or edit an AI-generated version. Independent judges rated the AI-generated messages as more effective, more affiliative and more explicit than the human ones.

This is, on its face, excellent news.

Dating apps have spent years asking human beings to advertise themselves using a handful of photographs, three amusing facts and whatever remains of their dignity after answering a prompt called “My most irrational fear.”

Now there is help.

Bumble introduced AI-generated profile guidance this year. The company says conversational, humorous or curiosity-provoking bios perform better than generic ones.

Tinder has gone further. Its AI-powered Chemistry system asks users questions and can, with permission, inspect their camera rolls for clues about interests, lifestyle and personality. It then uses those signals to recommend people.

So the machine can help decide what you should say about yourself, how you should say it and whom you should say it to.

There is only one remaining technical obstacle.

You.

Researchers are starting to find evidence of what happens when the optimized version of a person has to meet the original.

A study published in July interviewed 45 online daters who had either used large language models in romantic communication or encountered people who had. AI users often described the technology as helping them express what they genuinely meant. They didn't necessarily think they were pretending to be someone else.

Recipients sometimes saw things differently.

Discovering that romantic messages had been AI-assisted could feel deceptive. And users themselves described anxiety about eventually meeting in person, where the polished digital persona would have to survive without its co-author. The researcher called this the “Cyrano Effect.”

An even more uncomfortable study presented at CHI this year surveyed 113 online daters who had used AI-generated material in profiles or messages seen by people they later met in person. The researchers found cases where AI wasn't merely polishing expression but helping fabricate more attractive personalities, with disclosure uncommon partly because users feared romantic rejection.

The obvious conclusion is that AI makes catfishing easier.

That is true, but not terribly interesting. People were lying about their height before ChatGPT.

The stranger problem begins when nobody is exactly lying.

Imagine that you are funny, but not reliably funny. You ask AI for help with your profile and reject nine suggestions before finding one that sounds exactly like something you might have said on a particularly good day.

Is that fake?

Perhaps not.

Then someone matches with you because of it.

You use AI again when the conversation stalls. It doesn't invent your beliefs. It just notices an opening you missed, removes a nervous paragraph and suggests a joke that you alter enough to make your own.

Still you, presumably.

Soon you have produced a remarkably accurate representation of your personality under conditions in which your personality has been given unlimited editing time, an intelligent collaborator and a delete key.

Then Thursday arrives.

Thursday has cocktails.

Thursday has latency.

The other person says something unexpected and looks at you.

There is no regenerate button.

This reveals something dating profiles have always been doing besides describing people.

They sample them.

A clumsy joke is information. So is an awkward photograph, a boring answer, a message sent too quickly, an oddly specific enthusiasm for municipal trains. Imperfect self-presentation doesn't merely obstruct the search for a compatible person. Some of the imperfection is part of the evidence from which compatibility is inferred.

AI can improve the presentation while quietly changing what the presentation proves.

That distinction matters because people appear perfectly capable of preferring the improved output.

The experiment on date requests found AI messages objectively stronger. Other research complicates the idea that AI assistance automatically destroys interpersonal trust: in two preregistered experiments involving 1,637 participants playing trust games, people with predictive-text assistance produced equally trust-inducing messages in less time, even when AI use was disclosed.

AI assistance, in other words, can actually make communication better.

Which is precisely the problem.

If bad AI-written flirting were the issue, nature would take care of this for us.

Instead we are getting tools capable of improving the signal while potentially weakening its connection to the thing the signal supposedly measures.

Dating apps are discovering this at an awkward moment.

Their old machinery isn't exactly flourishing. Bumble reported that paying users fell 21 percent year over year in the first quarter of 2026; Bumble-app revenue fell 14 percent. Match Group's second-quarter results showed Tinder payers down 5 percent from a year earlier, even as Hinge continued growing.

The industry understandably wants better matching.

Bumble's chief executive has said the company intends to get rid of swiping. Tinder's Chemistry product promises fewer, more relevant recommendations rather than endless browsing.

But matching is only partly a problem of finding the correct person in a database.

It is also a problem of determining whether the person represented by the database survives contact with Tuesday night.

There is some evidence AI can help with even that. Researchers recently gave large language models transcripts from 964 speed dates and found that the models could detect modest signals of romantic attraction. Their predictions of actual matches were roughly competitive with human observers who had access to the same transcripts and added information beyond the daters' own predictions.

That is fascinating.

It also suggests a rather funny destination for a technology supposedly designed to make dating more human.

Your AI can improve your profile.

Their AI can improve theirs.

An AI can decide that you should meet.

Another AI can help you begin the conversation.

Eventually two heavily optimized representatives of two actual mammals agree to drinks.

The mammals arrive.

This doesn't mean AI has no place in dating. A dyslexic person getting help writing a profile hasn't committed romantic fraud. Neither has someone writing in a second language, someone nervous about texting, or somebody who simply has no idea what Bumble expects from “A pro and con of dating me.”

Human beings have always had assistance. Friends choose photographs. Sisters inspect messages. Entire group chats have spent Saturday nights determining whether “haha yeah definitely” means a man is interested.

The relevant line may not be assistance versus authenticity.

It may be whether the assistance preserves the predictive value of the encounter.

A friend helping you choose between two photographs is probably still showing another person what you look like. An AI constructing a charming conversational style you cannot reproduce has begun doing something different. The first improves transmission. The second may improve transmission by manufacturing evidence about the transmitter.

That is why the coming wave of AI dating tools may solve a problem and intensify another one at exactly the same time.

Dating apps have always contained uncertainty. Is this person as funny as the profile? Will we have anything to say? Are those photographs recent? Is this chemistry or merely two people who both know how to text?

AI can remove some of that uncertainty before the date.

But uncertainty does not simply disappear when software removes it from the screen.

Sometimes it moves.

And there is a name for the place where all the accumulated uncertainty eventually has to be settled.

The first date.
