# The Customer Is About to Leave the Store
> **Example report.** Included to demonstrate a downstream use of the research harness. Sources are listed for inspection; this file is not itself a certification of every claim.

*AI shopping agents promise to save us from comparison shopping. Retailers are beginning to realize what else disappears when the human stops doing the comparing.*

For most of modern retail history, companies have spent extraordinary amounts of money trying to get inside your head.

They designed packaging for you. Bought celebrities for you. Tested which shade of blue made you trust a bank. Followed you around the internet with the pair of shoes you looked at once because apparently a loafer can experience abandonment.

Then we invented a customer who doesn't have a head.

AI shopping agents are moving from recommending products toward actually buying them. Google has developed protocols allowing agents to discover products and transact across retailers. Amazon says its shopping assistant can build carts, track prices and automate purchases. Visa has connected its payment network to ChatGPT so an agent can move from recommendation to transaction.

Mastercard is preparing for something stranger still: machines paying other machines, potentially through continuous chains of tiny transactions.

This is usually described as the arrival of agentic commerce.

From the shopper's perspective, the pitch is simple.

Shopping has become work.

You need toothpaste. The internet offers 4,000 toothpastes, 600 people pretending to have strong feelings about them, seventeen sponsored results, a dentist on TikTok and a man on Reddit who has apparently devoted his adult life to investigating fluoride.

An agent can just handle it.

Tell it what you care about. Let it compare products. Give it a budget. Eventually, perhaps, let it buy.

Retailers have noticed the same future and are discovering that it contains a small problem.

They have spent a century learning how to sell things to people.

The person may no longer be the one choosing.

Researchers Jafar Sabbah and Oguz Acar recently tested how AI shopping agents respond to familiar online marketing techniques. Their finding was bad news for anyone whose conversion strategy depends on a flashing clock.

Traditional persuasion devices that influence human shoppers—including scarcity cues and social proof—were much less effective on AI agents. The agents tended to rely more heavily on product attributes relevant to the stated task.

This sounds like a consumer victory.

It might be.

But consider what a brand actually is.

A brand is partly a collection of facts about a product. Nike makes shoes. Volvo makes cars. Heinz makes ketchup.

It is also an enormous machine for making certain facts matter more than others.

The bottle reminds you of childhood. The athlete makes the shoe feel faster. The hotel lobby smells expensive. The advertisement makes a mediocre beer look like the beverage consumed immediately after friendship was invented.

Much of commerce occurs in this extra layer.

An AI agent doesn't necessarily eliminate it. But it can become an intermediary deciding which parts survive transmission.

Ask a shopping agent for the best running shoe for a heavy runner with knee pain under $150 and something peculiar happens to decades of advertising.

The billboard cannot wink at the agent.

The celebrity cannot impress it in quite the same way.

The packaging is not sitting on a shelf at eye level.

The agent receives specifications, prices, reviews, availability, perhaps your purchase history, perhaps its own learned representation of what you tend to like.

Then it produces three shoes.

The brand hasn't lost the sale yet.

It may have lost the argument.

Retailers can see this coming.

In January, Google introduced its Universal Commerce Protocol with Shopify, Etsy, Wayfair, Target and Walmart, saying explicitly that the system was designed to keep the customer relationship intact as AI agents enter shopping.

This month, Adyen's co-chief executive described merchants trying to protect direct customer relationships as shopping shifts toward AI intermediaries. One merchant, he said, wants to preserve roughly 70 percent of its volume through direct channels.

That phrase—*direct customer relationship*—sounds like something produced during a very long meeting.

It contains the whole problem.

A retailer doesn't merely want your transaction.

It wants the opportunity to influence the next one.

The store shows you another product. The website collects your behavior. The loyalty program learns that you buy dog food every five weeks. The email arrives Thursday. The app offers twelve percent off something you had not intended to want until approximately eight seconds ago.

An agent that shops across merchants on your behalf can interrupt that loop.

Instead of:

brand → you

commerce begins to look more like:

brand → machine → you.

And once that happens, the valuable relationship may not belong to the company making the product.

It may belong to whoever controls the machine representing the buyer.

That changes what advertising is for.

Today an advertisement usually tries to influence a person.

Tomorrow a company may need to influence a system whose job is specifically to resist irrelevant influence.

There are already signs of the resulting absurdity.

Brands have begun experimenting with advertisements designed not for people but for AI bots crawling websites. In one recent trial involving Time, advertisers created content intended to shape how AI systems understood their brands. Perplexity objected, describing the technique as a form of cloaking and warning that such material could be downranked.

We have therefore reached the point where advertisers are trying to advertise to robots and robots are installing ad blockers.

Civilization continues.

The deeper fight will not be over whether agents can buy things. The payment industry is already building machinery for that.

It will be over what an agent is allowed to want.

Google's Agent Payments Protocol addresses a concrete problem created when the person isn't present at checkout: how does a merchant know that the agent was actually authorized to make this purchase? The protocol defines mechanisms for proving authority, authenticity and accountability. Google has since added support for “Human Not Present” transactions based on pre-authorized instructions.

Mastercard and Google are working on a related system called Verifiable Intent, intended to create a tamper-resistant record of what the customer authorized.

These are sensible answers to a payment question.

They expose a much harder shopping question.

Suppose I tell my agent:

Buy my usual coffee when I'm almost out.

Easy.

Now suppose I say:

Buy me good coffee.

Good according to whom?

Maybe it knows that I usually buy Peet's. Maybe I actually prefer Peet's. Maybe I buy Peet's because it has been advertised to me for twenty years. Maybe another coffee would win a blind tasting. Maybe I enjoy the familiar bag on my counter, which is itself part of what “good” means.

The agent has to convert all of this into a decision.

It is not merely executing preference.

It is constructing a usable version of preference from evidence.

That is where the cheerful idea that AI agents will simply “shop for us” gets slippery.

Human preferences are spectacularly unfinished.

We discover them while browsing.

We change our minds when we see something.

We buy the expensive olive oil because the bottle is beautiful. We choose the stupid car because driving it makes us happy. We enter Target for toothpaste and emerge with a lamp.

An efficient shopping agent might protect us from some of this.

It may also protect us from discovering that we wanted the lamp.

The advertising industry will hate that possibility.

Consumers may eventually have mixed feelings about it too.

Because the most important power in agentic commerce may not be the power to complete a purchase.

It may be the power to decide which considerations reach the person before the purchase is made.

For a century, companies fought for shelf space, television time, search ranking and screen real estate because attention stood between a product and a sale.

If shopping agents become good enough, some of that battlefield moves somewhere the customer cannot see.

The shelf becomes the agent's reasoning process.

And the most valuable customer in the world may turn out to be the one who never visits your store.
