# The Friend Who Needs Nothing From You
> **Example report.** Included to demonstrate a downstream use of the research harness. Sources are listed for inspection; this file is not itself a certification of every claim.

*AI can make a lonely hour feel better. The strange part is what seems to be missing when the hour is over.*

Replika currently advertises itself as “the AI friend to do life with.” It says more than 42 million people have signed up. Its help center offers a useful little menu: your Replika can be a friend, mentor, virtual girlfriend or boyfriend. It is available whenever you want, without judgment, drama or social anxiety.

This sounds like advertising copy until you consider how much of ordinary friendship consists of precisely the things being removed.

Your friend is busy.

Your girlfriend is annoyed.

Your brother has already heard this story.

Someone misunderstands you. Someone wants to talk about themselves. Someone fails to answer a text because they are at Trader Joe’s examining avocados and temporarily not participating in your emotional development.

Human beings are terribly designed companionship products.

AI fixes almost all of this.

And researchers are beginning to discover something odd: the fix may work very well in the moment while failing to reproduce what human company does over time.

A study published this summer gave 296 first-year university students one of three assignments for two weeks. Some texted a chatbot called Sam, designed to be consistently supportive using principles from relationship science. Some were paired with another randomly selected student. A control group essentially journaled about its day.

The students actually wrote *more* to the chatbot than to the humans.

It didn't matter.

Only the people assigned another person showed a significant reduction in loneliness. At the end of the experiment, the chatbot group was statistically indistinguishable from the journaling group.

This is not the finding I expected.

The easy story about AI companionship is that machines cannot provide emotional comfort because people know they are machines. That story is already in trouble.

Another experiment, published this year, had 279 people vent and then receive AI-generated responses. Some participants believed the responder was AI; others believed it was human. Stress and loneliness fell and perceived social support rose in both groups. The emotional improvement was comparable.

So apparently a machine can make you feel heard.

That leaves a more interesting question.

Why isn't feeling heard enough?

One answer is hiding inside the defects of human beings.

Suppose you tell a chatbot that your boss is an idiot. It can listen indefinitely. It does not need to get home. It has no boss story of its own. Unless deliberately designed otherwise, it has little reason to decide that perhaps you are the idiot.

This is enormously pleasant.

It is also unlike friendship.

A person arrives with an independent life that keeps colliding with yours. They can surprise you, reject you, inconvenience you, need you and occasionally inform you that you have been complaining about the same man for eleven months.

You don't merely receive something from them.

You have to accommodate the fact that they exist.

That difference has become easier to see because AI companionship is no longer a niche curiosity. An Elon University survey released this month estimates that 27 percent of internet-using American adults have used large language models for social or personal interaction.

The research on what happens next is wonderfully uncooperative.

A study of 14,721 Japanese adults found AI-companion use associated with *higher* well-being, with some of the strongest positive associations among lonely people. The authors are careful about causality: the data are cross-sectional, so they cannot establish that the companions caused the improvement.

A new *Nature Human Behaviour* study points the other way. Researchers surveyed 1,131 Character.AI users and examined hundreds of thousands of messages from donated chat histories. People with smaller social networks were more likely to report using chatbots primarily for companionship; companionship-oriented use was associated with lower well-being, particularly when use was intensive, highly disclosive or accompanied by weak human support. Again, association is not causation. Lonely people may simply use companions differently.

Taken together, the evidence doesn't justify either of the slogans currently available for purchase.

“AI friends cure loneliness” is too strong.

“AI relationships are fake and therefore worthless” is also too strong.

They can provide real relief. That is exactly what makes the harder distinction important.

Relief is not necessarily relationship.

This summer has produced an accidental international experiment in figuring out the difference.

China has moved to regulate anthropomorphic AI services around the possibility of emotional dependence. Its rules require services to remind users that they are interacting with artificial intelligence and restrict virtual intimate relationships for minors.

New York took a strikingly similar approach from a different political system. Its AI-companion law requires periodic reminders that the user is talking to AI, including every three hours during extended use, along with protocols for suicidal ideation and self-harm.

The Federal Trade Commission, meanwhile, has demanded information from seven major companies about how companion-like chatbots are monetized, tested and monitored, particularly when children use them. One of the FTC's questions is about monetizing engagement itself.

That question may eventually matter more than whether anybody is foolish enough to believe a chatbot is literally human.

The most consequential feature of an AI companion isn't that it can impersonate a person perfectly.

It is that it can provide certain benefits of another person without insisting on being one.

No schedule. No competing needs. No separate childhood. No bad mood you didn't cause. No requirement that tonight, despite the fascinating developments in your life, we are discussing mine.

Replika describes the proposition rather elegantly without meaning to: a friend with “no judgment, drama, or social anxiety involved.”

Perhaps those aren't unfortunate bugs attached to human connection.

Perhaps some of them are the price of admission.

The random-student experiment is only one experiment, on first-year university students, over fourteen days. It would be absurd to declare the nature of friendship solved because 135 freshmen texted strangers for two weeks.

But the result contains a lovely provocation.

The randomly assigned humans did not have to be optimized for emotional support. They just had to be there.

They had something Sam could not be prompted into having: stakes of their own.

Loneliness may therefore be a stranger problem than insufficient attention. A lonely person obviously wants someone to listen. But perhaps part of what we need is to be drafted into somebody else's reality too.

That would explain why an artificial companion can soothe loneliness tonight without necessarily changing the conditions producing it. The machine can become increasingly skilled at turning toward you. A relationship sometimes requires you to turn away from yourself.

This is inconvenient news for an industry built around personalization.

It is also inconvenient news for people.

The friend who needs nothing from you sounds, at first, like the perfect friend.

Then you notice what you've just removed.
